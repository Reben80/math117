MATH 117   Unit 4 · The normal distribution

Probability is an area

The shaded area under the curve is the probability.
The whole curve has area 1, which is 100%.

SECTION 6.2

Prof. Rebin Muhammad

Montgomery College

Say: a probability is an area under the curve. Run your hand over the shade and call that the probability a value lands there. The whole curve has area 1, which is 100%. Half the bell is 0.50, or 50%. A probability is never bigger than 1. Standard deviation is the spread, the step from chapter 6.1. Use the word standard deviation first. cdf starts with a score and returns an area. inversecdf starts with an area and returns a score. Less-than and less-than-or-equal are the same area on a smooth curve. Say that only if someone asks. About 30 minutes. If you are short, stop after the golf scores. The phone ages can wait.

Example 6.7 · read a decimal as a percent

The two areas add to 100%

\(P(X > a) = 1 - P(X \le a)\)

The shade is 0.0228, or 2.28%. The rest is 1 − 0.0228 = 0.9772, or 97.72%.

Point at the shade: that small left piece is 0.0228, which is 2.28%. The unshaded rest of the curve is the complement, 1 − 0.0228 = 0.9772. Have them add the two decimals and get 1, the whole area. The line is z = −2. The book does not name an x for this picture. Write P(X > a) = 1 − P(X ≤ a) once. On a smooth curve, less-than and less-than-or-equal have the same area. Try It 6.7, only if asked: 0.012 is 1.2% on the left, so the right area is 0.988, or 98.8%. This is a probability: the shade is an area. A percentile question would hand back a score instead.

Example 6.8 · final exams, mean 63, sd 5

The probability of a score over 65

\(P(X > 65) = 1 - P(X \le 65) = 1 - 0.6554 = 0.3446\). About 34%. This is a probability. 69.4 and 65.6, later, are scores.

DESMOS ↗  normaldist(63, 5)  then Right from 65

The shade is the right tail, the probability of a score over 65. 65 is only 0.4 standard deviations above 63, so the area is not small. The area to the left is 0.6554. Subtract from 1: 0.3446, about 34 out of 100. In Desmos, type only normaldist(63, 5), then choose Right from 65. Choosing Left and forgetting to subtract gives 0.6554, the wrong side. 69.4 and 65.6 are not this answer. Those are percentiles, scores, on the later slide.

Two different questions

Start with a score, or start with an area

cdf is score → area, usually the area to the left. The same command also gives a right tail, or the area between two values. inversecdf is area → score.

Left graph: score → area. You know the score. The shade is the unknown probability. That is cdf, usually the left area. The same Desmos box also does a right tail, or the area between two values. Right graph: area → score. You know 90%. The score is the unknown edge. That is inversecdf. These pictures use no book data. Misconception: using inversecdf when the problem already gives the score.

Example 6.8 · the same exams

Finding a score from a percentile

What score has 90% of students at or below it? That score is 69.4, the 90th percentile. The 70th percentile is 65.6. These are scores, not probabilities.

DESMOS ↗  normaldist(63, 5).inversecdf(0.90)  gives the score 69.4

Ask: what score has 90% of students at or below it? Point at the shade, then at the edge. The shade is the 90% you were given. The edge, 69.4, is the score. That is the opposite of finding a probability. inversecdf(0.90) returns 69.4. The 70th percentile is inversecdf(0.70) = 65.6. One sentence in the book says 65.5. Use 65.6. If someone asks about a score over 85: that is a probability, about 1, because 85 is about four standard deviations above 63. It is not a percentile. Misconception: calling 69.4 a probability. Ask whether 69.4 is between 0 and 1. It is a score.

Your turn · Try It 6.8 · 3 minutes

The probability of a score under 65

Mean 68, standard deviation 3. What share of golfers score under 65?

65 is one standard deviation below 68. The shade is the probability to the left of 65.

DESMOS ↗  normaldist(68, 3)  then Left, to 65

Try It 6.8. Three minutes. The worked answer is the next slide. They type normaldist(68, 3), then Left to 65. A strong student sees z = (65−68)/3 = −1 first: one standard deviation below the mean, so the left tail is about 16%. The precise probability is 0.1587. If they report about 0.84, they found the area above 65. The score 65 is given, so this is a probability. inversecdf is the other question: the area is given and the score is unknown.

Try It 6.8

About 16%

$$z = \frac{65-68}{3} = -1$$

The probability is 0.1587, which is 15.87%.

Under this normal model, about 16% of golfers score below 65.

Point at 65, one standard deviation below 68. The shade is the left tail. The middle 68% leaves about 32% split into two tails, so each is about 16%. Desmos makes it 0.1587, or 15.87%. Say: under this normal model, about 16% of golfers score below 65. The precise probability is 0.1587. Ask them to move the decimal two places. The shade is the probability. A percentile question would have given about 16% and asked for the score.

Example 6.9 · hours a home computer is used

Mean 2 hours, standard deviation 0.5

First the probability: the book’s area between 1.8 and 2.75 is 0.5886, about 59%. Then a cutoff: the first quartile is 1.66 hours, with 25% of households at or below it.

DESMOS ↗  normaldist(2, 0.5)  then Inner, 1.8 to 2.75

DESMOS ↗  normaldist(2, 0.5).inversecdf(0.25)  gives the quartile 1.66

Do the interval first. The shade between 1.8 and 2.75 is a probability, 0.5886, about 59 out of 100 households. Type normaldist(2, 0.5), then Inner from 1.8 to 2.75. The two hours go in the area box. The mean and standard deviation go inside normaldist. Only after that, the quartile. inversecdf(0.25) is a cutoff, 1.66 hours. A quarter of the households are at or below it. Try It 6.9, only if asked: golf again, between 66 and 70, area 0.4950. Misconception: typing the hours 2 and 0.5 as the interval.

Example 6.11 · smartphone users, ages 13 to 55+

Mean age 36.9, standard deviation 13.9

The shaded half runs from Q1 = 27.52 to Q3 = 46.28. The IQR is 18.8 years. Forty percent are 40.4 or older, so 60% are to the left. Use inversecdf(0.60).

DESMOS ↗  normaldist(36.9, 13.9).inversecdf(0.60)  gives about 40.4

This slide can wait if the hour is gone. The shade is the middle half, from the first quartile to the third. The book prints Q1 = 27.5246 and Q3 = 46.2754. The graph rounds those to 27.52 and 46.28. The difference is 18.75, and the book rounds the IQR to 18.8 years. The 40.4 question is separate. Forty percent are at least 40.4, so the area to the left of that age is 60%. inversecdf(0.60) = 40.4215, and the book rounds to 40.4. The trap is typing 0.40. That returns the age with 40% younger, the wrong side. Q1, Q3, and 40.4 are cutoffs, so use inversecdf. A probability question would start with an age and return an area.
