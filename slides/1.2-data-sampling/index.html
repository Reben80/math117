Section 1.2

Two questions

01

What kind of data?

Qualitative, or quantitative. If it is quantitative, discrete or continuous.

02

How was the sample chosen?

Simple random, stratified, cluster, systematic, or convenience.

A statistic is only useful when the sample looks like the population.

The kind of data

Words, or numbers

QUALITATIVE

A category

Hair color, blood type, the car someone drives. Described by words or letters. An average hair color does not make sense. A letter grade, A through F, is qualitative too.

QUANTITATIVE

A number

Money, pulse, weight, the number of students in a class. Quantitative data come from counting or measuring. The next slide splits them in two.

Book note: quiz scores can be collected as numbers and reported as A, B, C, D, or F. The scores are quantitative. The letters are qualitative.

Quantitative data

Count, or measure

DISCRETE

A count

Phone calls in a day: 0, 1, 2, or 3. Only certain numbers are possible. You do not receive 2.4 calls.

CONTINUOUS

A measurement

Length of a call: 2.4, 7.5, or 11.0 minutes. Fractions and decimals are allowed. Length, weight, and time are the usual examples.

A phrase that starts with “the number of” is usually discrete.

Example 1.5 is books in a backpack: 3, 4, 2, 1, discrete. Example 1.6 is the weights: 6.2, 7, 6.8, 9.1, 4.3, continuous.

Qualitative data

Three ways to show it

01

Pie chart

Each wedge is a category. The size matches the percent. The wedges have to add to 100%.

02

Bar graph

The length of the bar matches the count or the percent. Bars can be vertical or horizontal.

03

Pareto chart

A bar graph sorted from largest to smallest. Sorting makes the biggest category obvious.

Example 1.10: a pie of first-years, sophomores, juniors, and seniors is qualitative data. There is no single best graph. It depends on the question.

When a pie chart fails

The pieces have to make one whole

MORE THAN 100%

150.5%

Full-time 40.9%. Intend to transfer 48.6%. Under age 25, 61.0%. A student can be in all three. Use a bar graph.

LESS THAN 100%

90.4%

The ethnicity table left out Other/Unknown. The listed groups are not the whole college. Put the missing group back, or use a bar graph.

A pie chart needs every category, and each person in only one of them.

Sampling

Five ways to choose

01
Simple random
Every group of the same size is equally likely. Put the names in a hat.
02
Stratified
Split into groups. Take some people from every group.
03
Cluster
Split into groups. Pick some groups, and take everyone in them.
04
Systematic
Start at random, then take every nth person. Every 50th name in a list of 20,000.
05
Convenience
Whoever is easy to reach. Shoppers already in the store. This one can be biased.
In a random sample, each person starts with an equal chance. Convenience is the one that is not random. The book’s calculator demo is randInt(0, 30) for a class of 31. Skip the button sequence unless someone asks.

The mix-up

Some from every group,
or everyone from some groups

STRATIFIED

Some from each

25 first-years, 25 sophomores, 25 juniors, 25 seniors. Every class year is in the sample.

CLUSTER

All from some

Pick two class years at random. Then take every student in those two years. The other years are out.

Same groups, opposite move. Stratified: a few from all groups. Cluster: all of a few groups.

Replacement

Can the same person
be picked twice?

WITH REPLACEMENT

The name goes back

That person can be chosen again. This is the theoretical random sample.

WITHOUT REPLACEMENT

The name stays out

Surveys are done this way. For a large population and a small sample, the two are nearly the same.

The difference matters when the population is small.

What can go wrong

Three different problems

01

Sampling error

Comes from using a sample. A larger sample makes it smaller. It never disappears.

02

Nonsampling error

A bad question, a broken counter, a missed person. A larger sample does not fix it.

03

Bias

Some people are less likely to be chosen. A large biased sample is still biased. Call-in surveys are biased.

Mailed surveys that come back on their own favor whoever bothers to answer. Better if the researcher selects who is asked.

Critical evaluation

Before you trust the study

01

Representative

A sample that misses part of the population gives results that are not valid.

02

Self-selected

Call-in surveys and volunteers. People choose to respond.

03

Sample size

Small samples can mislead. Crash tests and rare conditions are the exception.

04

Nonresponse

The people who answer often have the strong opinions.

05

The wording

A question can push the answer. The book calls this undue influence.

06

Cause

Two things can move together because of a third. Related is not caused.

07

Who paid

Read a self-interest study on the work. Do not assume it is good or bad.

08

Confounding

Several factors are tangled, so you cannot separate their effects.

The book also lists misleading graphs: a bad scale, missing categories, no context. Discuss if time: honor students are a bad sample for the campus GPA. Every 20th child at one supermarket is not all children under 10. Twenty people in Central Park are not commuters in general. A simple random sample of 100 Massachusetts hospitals does match a question about hospitals in that state.

Example 1.13

What part-time students spend on books

ABC College. 10,000 part-time students. Ten people in each sample.

01

Chemistry class

Convenience. Several are also in calculus. Book bills run high. Biased.

02

Every fifth senior in a PE class

Systematic, but only senior citizens. Book bills run low. Biased.

03

One student from each of 10 fields

Simple random inside each field. Unbiased, and still small.

A bigger sample does not repair a biased method.

Chemistry amounts: 128, 87, 173, 116, 130, 204, 147, 189, 93, 153. Senior PE: 50, 40, 36, 15, 50, 100, 40, 53, 22, 22. Ten fields: 180, 50, 150, 85, 260, 75, 180, 200, 200, 150. The third sample is unbiased. The book still wants a larger sample.
