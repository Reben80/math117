The middle 95% of those proportions is the interval.

The medical consultant

Parameter

The true complication rate for this consultant’s clients. The sample does not hand you p.

Statistic

p-hat = 0.048

3 divided by 62. The point estimate. The book writes 0.048.

A comparison

About 10%

The national rate for liver-donor surgeries. That 10% is not p.

The data are observational. A lower rate does not, by itself, show that her work caused it.

DESMOS ↗  3/62  the point estimate 0.048

The book’s case: one consultant, 3 complications in 62 liver-donor surgeries. p is the true complication rate for her clients. p-hat is 3 divided by 62, which the book writes as 0.048. That is the statistic, the point estimate. The national average is about 10%. That number is a comparison. It is not the parameter. The claim that her work caused the lower rate does not follow. Patients who hire a consultant may also have other advantages. Say confounding once, in that plain sentence, and move on. The likely mistake is treating 0.048 as the true rate, or treating 10% as the center of the interval.

Sampling with replacement

Draw 62 records, putting each one back

The bag

59 and 3

59 records with no complication, and 3 with a complication. The bag is the sample.

One resample

62 draws

Each draw is replaced. A record can appear more than once. The size stays 62.

Then again

10,000 times

Each resample has its own proportion. One proportion is not the interval.

A single simulation lands near 0.048, the rate in the original sample. The next one moves a little. The book uses the pile of 10,000.

There are 59 surgeries with no complication and 3 with a complication. A bootstrap draw picks one of the 62 records and puts it back, then does that 62 times. Because the record goes back, the same patient can be drawn twice. That is sampling with replacement. The sample size stays 62. One resample produces one bootstrap proportion, written p-hat boot. The book says a single simulation is about 4.8%, with a little variation. The book then repeats the simulation 10,000 times. The likely mistake is treating the first resample as the confidence interval.

The book’s 10,000 resamples

The 95% interval is 0 to 0.113

0 to 0.113 is 0% to 11.3% of patients. 10% is inside the interval.

The interval does not show that her rate is below the national 10%.

The book’s figure: 10,000 bootstrap proportions, centered on 3/62. The 2.5 percentile is 0 and the 97.5 percentile is 0.113. The 95% percentile interval is those two ends: 0 to 0.113, which is 0% to 11.3%. The ends are shares of patients, not counts of complications. Point at 10%. It sits inside 0 to 11.3%. So the interval does not show that the consultant’s rate is under the national rate. It might be lower, and it might be higher. Also say again: even a rate below 10% would not, from these data, show that her work caused it. The likely mistake is reading 0.113 as 113 patients, or as 11.3 complications.

The tapper and the listener

3 of 120 listeners guessed the song

The point estimate is 3/120 = 0.025. One of those ten resamples is 2/120 = 0.0167.

The book’s 95% interval, from 10,000 resamples, is 0 to 0.0583. That is 0% to 5.83%. 50% is far outside it.

Elizabeth Newton’s study, as the book tells it: 120 listeners, and 3 guessed the tapped song. p-hat is 3/120 = 0.025. The book lists ten bootstrap proportions: 0.0417, 0.025, 0.025, 0.0083, 0.05, 0.0333, 0.025, 0, 0.0083, and 0. The dots are those ten. Duplicates stack. One of them is 2 correct out of 120, which is 0.0167. That single value is a bootstrap proportion, not the interval. The book then runs 10,000 resamples. The 2.5 percentile is 0 and the 97.5 percentile is 0.0583. The 95% interval is 0 to 5.83%. Tappers expected about 50%. 50% is not in the interval, and it is far from the largest resample the book describes. The likely mistake is averaging the ten dots and calling that the interval.
