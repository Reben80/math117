One sample, one average

Three samples, three sample means

90

Five students. One sample mean.

SAMPLE 2

87

Five students. One sample mean.

SAMPLE 3

93

Five students. One sample mean.

The population mean is one fixed center. Each sample of five students gives one sample mean. Many sample means together form the sampling distribution.

Say: take five students and average them. That one number is a sample mean. A different five students give a different sample mean, 87 or 93 instead of 90. Point at the three dots. Those averages are not the population. If we kept taking samples, the pile of averages is the sampling distribution. Misconception: calling 90 the population mean. On this slide 90 is just one sample's average. The population mean is the center those averages scatter around.

Two different distributions

Same center, smaller spread

Mean \(\mu\). Standard deviation \(\sigma\).

Mean \(\mu\). Standard error \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\).

These curves are sketches. The original population need not be normal. The Central Limit Theorem says the sample means are approximately normal, and a larger n makes them less variable.

Say the two names before any formula. Left is one observation, X, with population mean mu and population standard deviation sigma. Right is the sample mean, X-bar, from samples of 25. Same center. The spread on the right is the standard error, sigma over the square root of n. Say the curves are sketches, not a promise that the population is a bell. Larger n makes that spread smaller. The Central Limit Theorem is why the right-hand curve is approximately a bell even when the population is not. If the population is badly skewed, 25 may not be enough. Misconception: students think the population itself got narrower. It did not. Only the distribution of the averages did.

Standard error of the mean

Divide by the square root of n

STANDARD ERROR

$$\sigma_{\bar{X}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$

The typical spread of the sample means. \(\sigma\) is the population standard deviation. n is the sample size.

Z-SCORE FOR A SAMPLE MEAN

$$z = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}}$$

Same move as section 6.1. The bottom is the standard error, which you can think of as the step.

THE BOOK’S CHECK

\(\mu = 90,\; \sigma = 15,\; n = 25\)

15 / √25 = 3

The standard error is 3, not the population standard deviation 15.

90 + 2(3) = 96

Two standard errors above the mean is 96. The center stays 90.

Say standard error of the mean before you say step. It measures how much sample means vary from one sample to the next. n is the sample size, not the number of times the class repeats the experiment. On the board: 15 divided by the square root of 25 is 15 divided by 5, which is 3. Then 90 + 2(3) = 96. Read once: X-bar is approximately normal with mean mu and standard deviation sigma over the square root of n. Misconception: dividing by 25 instead of by 5. That gives 0.6. Another: moving the center to 90/25. The center stays 90.

Example 7.1 · an unknown distribution

Mean 90, standard deviation 15, n = 25

\(P(85 < \bar{X} < 92) = 0.6997\)

Standard error 15/√25 = 3. About 70 of 100 sample means land between 85 and 92.

The population shape is unknown, so this is a normal approximation.

DESMOS  normaldist(90, 15/sqrt(25))  then Inner, 85 to 92

Say: the population can be any shape. With n = 25, the sample means are approximately normal, centered at the population mean 90, with standard error 3. Type only the monospace command. Desmos opens the area box. Choose Inner, and set 85 and 92. Desmos shows 0.69971711018. The book rounds to 0.6997. Misconception: normaldist(90, 15). That is one observation, the chapter 6 bell. The sample size has to get into the standard error. Part b of the example is the 96 from the previous slide.

Before Tuesday

Three facts about sample means

The mean of the sampling distribution equals the population mean.

The standard error is the population standard deviation divided by √n.

Sample means get closer to a normal curve as n grows. A skewed population needs a larger n.
