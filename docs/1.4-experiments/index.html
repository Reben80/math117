Section 1.4

Same health difference, two studies

Random assignment spreads diet, exercise, and smoking across both groups.

An observational study compares groups that already exist. An experiment assigns the treatment at random, so lurking variables are spread across the groups. Only then can a difference in the response be tied to the treatment.

The words

What an experiment names

Explanatory
The variable the researcher sets. Here, which pill.
Response
What gets measured afterward. A heart attack, yes or no.
Treatments
The values of the explanatory variable. Aspirin, and a placebo.
Experimental unit
One person, or one object, that gets measured.
Lurking variable
Something else that differs between the groups and can explain the result.

The power of suggestion

A pill can work because you expect it to

Believing you had taken a performance drug produced times almost as fast as actually taking it.

Placebo

Looks like the treatment. It cannot itself change the response.

Control group

Gets the placebo, so being in the study is the same for everyone.

Blinding

The person does not know who received the active treatment.

Double-blind

The subjects and the researchers who meet them are both blinded.

McClung and Collins, 2007, quoted in the section. Taking the drug without knowing produced no significant gain. Blinding keeps that expectation equal in both groups. If people know they got the empty pill, suggestion drops out.

Example 1.19

Does aspirin reduce heart attacks?

Population

People aged 50 to 84

Sample

The 400 who joined

Experimental unit

One person

Explanatory

Oral medication

Treatments

Aspirin or a placebo

Response

A heart attack, yes or no

One pill a day for three years. They do not know which pill. The subjects are blinded.

The book’s response variable is whether a subject had a heart attack. Researchers count how many in each group. Try It 1.19, medicines A, B, and C and adult height, is the same six labels if you want a second pass. Height will not change in six months. That is a useful aside, not a slide.

Example 1.20

Can a scent change the time?

EXPLANATORY

The scent of the mask

RESPONSE

Time to finish the maze

Treatments: a floral mask and an unscented mask. Everyone wears both, in a random order.

Subjects can smell the flowers, so they cannot be blinded. The person with the stopwatch can be.

Random order spreads any practice effect. Lurking variables are not a group difference here, because every subject gets both treatments. Researchers timing the maze can be blinded. Subjects cannot.

Example 1.21

Birth order cannot be assigned

You cannot hand someone a birth order.

First-born and later-born children already differ in other ways. Those differences stay mixed in. The study stays observational.

When random assignment is impossible, a response difference cannot be pinned on the explanatory variable alone. Texting and driving, Try It 1.21, can be an experiment: you can assign who texts.

Example 1.22

Three ways to spoil the data

a
A comfortable block
A convenient sample can be biased. It does not stand for the community.
b
Skipped empty houses
People who are at work never get counted. Relevant data is missing.
c
Filled in the forms later
Copying other residents’ answers is faking data. That is never acceptable.

Human subjects need informed consent, in writing. An institutional review board approves the study before it starts.

Stapel, for you only if a student asks: he created data sets, altered data, and misreported how many subjects he had. More than 55 papers. Do not make that a slide. IRB: risks minimized and reasonable against the benefits, consent documented, individual data kept private.
